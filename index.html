What Is Brickbreaker

The brick breaker genre started in 1976 when Atari shipped Breakout. Steve Wozniak built the hardware in four days — the circuit was so compact Atari's engineers had to redesign it for production. The game was simple: bounce a ball off a paddle to smash rows of colored bricks. Breakout sold about 11,000 arcade cabinets. Almost every game in the genre since 1976 copies its core design.

In 1986, Taito's Arkanoid added power-ups that dropped from destroyed bricks — lasers, multi-ball, wider paddles — plus end-of-stage bosses. It introduced the "capsule catch" mechanic: move under falling items to grab them before they leave the screen. Arkanoid became the most influential brick breaker after Breakout.

On home computers, DX-Ball (1996) dominated Windows shareware for years. Its creator, Michael P. Welch, built it as a hobby project that reached millions. Nintendo packed Alleyway with the Game Boy at launch. The 2000s brought thousands of Flash variants to sites like Miniclip and Kongregate. BlackBerry preloaded its own brick breaker on every device, introducing touch-controlled paddle play to a generation of mobile users.

Tips to Score Higher

6 Brick Breaker Techniques

These tips are based on how the physics and scoring actually work:

  • Use Edge Hits to Aim — The paddle creates different bounce angles depending on where the ball lands. Left edge = left diagonal. Right edge = right diagonal. Center = straight up. Use this to pick off bricks in corners.
  • Target Gold Bricks First — At 50 points each, gold bricks are worth 5× a normal brick. Find them at the start of each level and clear them before the ball speed increases.
  • Multi-Ball Is Strong But Risky — Three balls clear bricks fast, but tracking all three is harder. You only lose a life when the last ball drops. Two balls still in play means you're still alive.
  • Use Steel Bricks to Bounce — Gray steel bricks can't break. Bounce the ball off them deliberately to reach spots your paddle angle can't hit directly.
  • Stay Centered, Move Less — Chasing the ball side to side causes misses. Keep the paddle near the middle and make small adjustments. From center, you can reach the full width with minimal movement.
  • Watch Shadows, Not the Ball — Past level 20, tracking the ball directly gets hard. Focus on the brick shadows and paddle position. Use peripheral vision to judge the ball's path. This alone can stop you from losing a ball on fast levels.

History of Brick Breaker Games

Brick breaker is one of the oldest video game genres still played today. Most modern versions trace their design back to three or four landmark titles:

1976 — Breakout (Atari)

Nolan Bushnell wanted a single-player Pong. He gave the project to Steve Jobs, who brought in Steve Wozniak to design the circuit board. Wozniak worked nonstop for four nights and built a chip with just 44 ICs — so compact Atari's engineers couldn't understand it and had to redesign for mass production. Breakout sold 11,000 cabinets and created the template for every paddle-and-brick game that followed.

1986 — Arkanoid (Taito)

Taito added power-up capsules that drop from destroyed bricks — laser, multi-ball, wide paddle — plus a boss at each stage. The catch mechanic created a split-second risk/reward decision: move for the power-up and risk missing the ball? Arkanoid sold 200,000 boards in Japan and spawned sequels across consoles, PCs, and mobile that are still sold today.

1989–1996 — Home Gaming Boom

Alleyway shipped with the Game Boy in 1989 as a launch title — Nintendo's take on the format featured Mario at the controls. DX-Ball (1996) became one of the most downloaded Windows shareware titles of the decade, proving the genre had a massive audience outside of arcades.

2000s — Flash and Mobile

Adobe Flash brought thousands of brick breaker variants to web portals. BlackBerry preloaded Brick Breaker on every phone — millions of people played their first brick breaker on a BlackBerry.

Today — HTML5 Canvas

Modern browsers render games at 60fps with hardware acceleration — something Flash never managed. This brick breaker runs on the same HTML5 stack. No plugins, no compatibility issues — just open and play.
