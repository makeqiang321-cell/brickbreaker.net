What Is Brickbreaker

The brick breaker genre started in 1976 when Atari shipped Breakout. Steve Wozniak designed the hardware in four days — a circuit so compact Atari's own engineers had to redo it for mass production. The game was simple: bounce a ball off a paddle to smash rows of colored bricks. It sold about 11,000 arcade cabinets. Nearly every brick breaker released since 1976 copies Breakout's core design.

In 1986, Taito's Arkanoid added power-ups that dropped from destroyed bricks — lasers, multi-ball, wider paddles — plus end-of-stage bosses. The game introduced the "capsule catch" mechanic: you had to move under falling items to grab them before they left the screen. Arkanoid became the most influential game in the genre after Breakout.

On home computers, DX-Ball (1996) dominated Windows shareware downloads for years. Its creator, Michael P. Welch, built it as a hobby project — it went on to reach millions of players. Nintendo put Alleyway on the Game Boy at launch. The 2000s brought thousands of Flash variants to sites like Miniclip and Kongregate, while BlackBerry preloaded its own version on every device, introducing touch-controlled paddle play to a generation of mobile users.

Six techniques that make a difference in this brick breaker, based on how the physics and scoring actually work:

History of Brick Breaker Games

Brick breaker is one of the oldest video game genres still played today. Most modern brick breaker games trace their design back to the same three or four landmark titles. The major releases that shaped it:

1976 — Breakout (Atari)

Atari's Nolan Bushnell wanted a single-player Pong variant. He assigned the project to a young Steve Jobs, who brought in Steve Wozniak to design the circuit board. Wozniak worked nonstop for four nights and produced a chip design so compact — just 44 ICs — that Atari's own engineers couldn't understand it. They had to redesign the board for mass production. Despite the chaos behind the scenes, Breakout sold 11,000 arcade cabinets and created the template for every paddle-and-brick game that followed.

1986 — Arkanoid (Taito)

Taito added power-up capsules that drop from destroyed bricks — laser, multi-ball, wide paddle — plus a boss enemy at the end of each stage. The "catch the falling item" mechanic was new and created a split-second risk/reward decision: do you move for the power-up and risk missing the ball? Arkanoid sold 200,000 arcade boards in Japan alone and spawned a franchise spanning consoles, PCs, and mobile ports that are still sold today.

1989–1996 — Home Gaming Boom

Alleyway shipped with the Game Boy in 1989 as a launch title — Nintendo's take on the format featured Mario at the controls. DX-Ball (1996) became one of the most downloaded Windows shareware titles of the decade, proving the genre had a massive audience outside of arcades. Both titles kept the core loop simple: move the paddle, keep the ball alive, clear the screen.

2000s — Flash and Mobile

Adobe Flash brought thousands of brick breaker variants to web portals. BlackBerry preloaded Brick Breaker on every phone — millions of people played their first brick breaker on a BlackBerry.

Today — HTML5 Canvas

Modern browsers render games at 60fps with hardware acceleration — something Flash never managed. This brick breaker runs on that same HTML5 stack. No plugins, no compatibility issues — this brick breaker just opens and plays.
