BlackBerry Brick Breaker — The Phone Game That Defined a Generation

Published August 12, 2026 by BrickBreaker.net

Before the iPhone. Before the App Store. Before "mobile gaming" was even a phrase people used — there was a preloaded game on every BlackBerry that nobody asked for and everybody played. It was called Brick Breaker, and it turned millions of businesspeople into gamers without them ever noticing.

You opened your BlackBerry to check email. Forty-five minutes later you were still there, trackball thumbed raw, trying to clear level 28. You told yourself you were just killing time before the next meeting. You were lying.

This is the story of the game that defined mobile gaming before mobile gaming existed — who built it, why it worked, and where you can play its spiritual successors today.

2003 — Waterloo, Ontario

The Solo RIM Developer Behind Brick Breaker

BlackBerry Brick Breaker shipped preloaded on every BlackBerry device starting in 2003. It was developed internally at Research In Motion (RIM) — the company behind BlackBerry — by a programmer named Arvin Dsouza.

Arvin Dsouza's Trackball Demo

Dsouza built the game as a way to show off the new trackball input on the BlackBerry 8100 Pearl. The brief was simple: make something that makes people want to use the trackball. Make it intuitive. Make it addictive. Make it so good that people forget they are holding a work device.

Why Brick Breaker Fit the Trackball

He chose the brick breaker format because it needed only one axis of input — left and right. The trackball was perfect for it. More precise than a D-pad, more tactile than a touchscreen. Move your thumb a millimeter to the right, the paddle slides. Flick it, the paddle snaps. The feedback loop was immediate and physical in a way that touchscreen controls still struggle to replicate.

The game shipped with 34 levels, each one progressively harder. By the time you hit level 20, the ball was moving at speeds that required peripheral vision — you stopped looking at the paddle and started tracking the ball's trajectory instead. That was the moment people got hooked. That was the moment a business phone became a gaming device.

Gameplay

34 Levels and One Trackball

The Classic 6-Color Row System

The BlackBerry version used a classic row-based brick layout — red on top, then orange, yellow, green, blue, and purple at the bottom. Higher rows were harder to reach but paid more points. Same scoring logic as Atari Breakout, same tension as the arcade original.

Trackball Precision vs. Touchscreen Drift

The key difference was the trackball. Unlike a mouse or a touchscreen, the trackball had physical resistance. You could feel the paddle move. After a few hours of play, your thumb developed muscle memory — you knew exactly how much roll translated to how much paddle shift. It was the closest thing to an analog joystick that a phone has ever had.

Power-Ups: Laser, Multi-Ball, and Catch

Power-ups appeared randomly from destroyed bricks. The most valuable was the Laser — two vertical beams that sliced through bricks above the paddle. Multi-Ball split your ball into three. Catch let you hold the ball on the paddle and aim your release. These capsules turned a game of reflexes into a game of decision-making — do I chase the power-up or stay in position to catch the ball?

The game had 34 hand-designed levels. No procedural generation, no random layouts. Every single brick position was placed by a human being who wanted you to figure out the one right angle that clears a stubborn corner. Levels 1–10 were warmups. Levels 11–20 separated the casual players from the committed ones. Levels 21–34 required pattern recognition, angle geometry, and a thumb that did not cramp.

Cultural Impact

The Accidental First Mobile Game for Millions

BlackBerry Brick Breaker was not on an app store because there was no app store. It was not "downloaded" or "installed" — it was just there, hiding in the Games folder next to Word Mole and Texas Hold'em King. The only reason you found it was because you were bored on a flight or waiting for a conference call to start.

Viral Before Viral Was a Thing

And then you told a coworker. And they told their spouse. And suddenly everyone with a BlackBerry — which, in 2007, was basically everyone in an office — was playing the same game. It was the closest thing to a viral hit that the pre-smartphone world could produce. No share button, no leaderboard, no "invite a friend." Just word of mouth and a trackball.

From Airport Gates to Air Force One

The game's cultural footprint is surprisingly deep. Barack Obama famously used a BlackBerry throughout his presidency and reportedly played Brick Breaker on Air Force One. Business travelers swapped level strategies at airport gates like schoolkids trading Pokemon tips. IT departments fielded support tickets about "my trackball is not responsive" when the real problem was that someone had worn it down playing three hours of Brick Breaker in a hotel room.

When the iPhone launched in 2007 and the App Store followed in 2008, BlackBerry Brick Breaker effectively died. RIM never ported it to touchscreens in a meaningful way. The game that defined mobile gaming for half a decade was abandoned by the platform that created it — and the iPhone's tilt-controlled, swipe-based games filled the void before anyone had time to mourn.

Play Today

Play BlackBerry-Style Brick Breaker Today

The original BlackBerry Brick Breaker cannot be played today — not on a real device, at least. RIM shut down BlackBerry OS services years ago, and even if you find a working BlackBerry on eBay, the game is tied to firmware that no modern network supports.

The DNA Lives On in HTML5

But the game's DNA lives on in brick breaker games today. The same 6-color row system. The same power-up logic. The same progressively faster ball speed. Modern HTML5 versions run at 60fps with the same paddle-and-ball physics that made the BlackBerry version feel so tight. The difference is that they work on any device — phone, tablet, laptop — with no app store and no download.
