Hide in plain sight. Move for the fireflies. Stay still for your life.

Mottle is a camouflage hide-and-seek game where the whole game runs on a single trade-off: moving reveals you, and standing still hides you. You control a small creature on a colored arena, and your job is to collect fireflies for points while the seekers sweep the ground with searchlights. Every firefly forces you to move, and every move is a risk.

The controls are about as small as a game gets. Drag with the mouse or your finger to move, and release to stop. The instant you release, your character blends into the color of the ground beneath it — so where you stop matters as much as when you stop. Esc pauses, and that is the entire control set.

The camouflage is the whole game. You are never truly invisible; you are only as hidden as your color is close to the patch of ground you stopped on. A light can pass straight over you and miss, as long as you are still and the colors match. Start moving again and you pop back into view, vivid and obvious to anything watching.

The seekers are not other players — they are the arena's searchlights, the alert meter, and the clock. As the rounds go on they get faster, the lights sweep more aggressively, and the fireflies you are chasing sit further apart across open ground. The result is a quiet, tense survival game where the best move is often no move at all.

To be honest about its limits: there is no combat, no levels, and no story. It is one mechanic — hide or be seen — repeated and tightened until it becomes genuinely tense. As a short, free, browser game about patience and timing, it does exactly what it sets out to do.

Mottle Strategy — Survive Longer Every Round

Short dashes, matched colors, and patience over greed.

Move in short dashes, not long runs. The single biggest cause of getting caught is trying to cross too much open ground in one go. Break every trip into the smallest hops you can — dash from one hiding spot to the next, stop, and reassess. A long run keeps you visible the whole way; three short dashes only expose you three times for a second each.

Pick your stopping spot by color before you move. Camouflage is only as good as the ground you stop on, so read the arena and choose a patch that matches your character before you start moving toward it. Stopping on a mismatched color is the same as staying visible, and it is the second most common way to get caught.

Time your movement to the searchlights. The lights sweep in a pattern, and the gap between two passes is your window. Move as soon as a light has passed over you, and be stopped and blended before the next one arrives. Standing still while a light passes over a matching color is the safest position in the game — learn to sit in it and wait.

Do not chase every firefly. Fireflies are bait, and some of them are deliberately placed across wide-open ground where the points are not worth the exposure. Let the risky ones go and take the ones near your hiding spots instead. Surviving the round is worth more than one greedy grab that gets you caught.

Watch the Alert meter like it is the score. It is your early warning — if it is climbing, you are overexposed, and if it is settling, you are safe. Build the habit of glancing at it after every dash, and treat a rising meter as a signal to stop and hide, not a reason to speed up.

What Makes Mottle Different

It is hide-and-seek where the hiding is your own color.

The first difference is that you camouflage by color, not by turning into a prop. Most hide-and-seek games let you become a lamp or a crate; Mottle takes the opposite approach — you are always you, and your only disguise is standing still on a matching patch of ground. That makes the hiding honest: there is no perfect disguise, only better or worse spots.

The second is that movement itself is the danger. In most stealth games the risk is being seen doing something wrong; in Mottle the risk is simply being in motion. That flips the usual incentive — the game rewards doing nothing, and the most skilled players are the ones who know when not to act.

The third is the pace. There is no map to learn and no enemy AI to outsmart, just searchlights that sweep faster as the rounds climb. The tension comes from a clean, escalating rhythm rather than a complex system, which makes it instantly readable and quietly addictive.

Put it together and you get a stealth game stripped down to a single decision, made over and over: move now, or wait. It is not trying to be a big survival sim — it is a small, tense game about the value of standing perfectly still, and it lands that idea cleanly.

Mottle Online — FAQ

Real answers to the questions players actually search — controls, camouflage, fireflies, the Alert meter, getting caught, mobile, and multiplayer.

How do you play Mottle, and what are the controls?

Mottle is a camouflage hide-and-seek game: you control a small creature on a colored arena, and your goal is to collect fireflies while staying hidden from the seekers. Drag with the mouse or your finger to move, and release to stop. The moment you release, your character blends into the color of the ground beneath it. Esc pauses the game. There are no other buttons — moving and stopping are the entire control set.

How does camouflage work in Mottle?

Camouflage is the core mechanic: as long as you are moving, you are bright and easy for the seekers to spot, but the instant you release and stand still, your character fades into the ground color beneath it. You are never invisible — you are only as hidden as your color is close to the patch of ground you stopped on. That means the safest place to stop is a patch of ground that matches you, and the worst place is a color that makes you stand out.

How do you collect fireflies in Mottle, and what are they for?

Fireflies are the points. You collect them by dragging your character over them, which means every firefly forces you to move — and moving is exactly what makes you visible. That is the whole tension: each firefly is bait that asks you to trade safety for points. There is no way to collect them while camouflaged, so the game is a series of short, deliberate dashes from one hiding spot to the next, with a firefly as the payoff.

What is the Alert meter in Mottle, and how do you avoid the seekers?

The Alert meter shows how close the seekers are to noticing you. It rises while you move and settles back down while you are still and well camouflaged. The seekers sweep the arena with searchlights, and the key is to time your movement to the gaps between those sweeps — move only when a light has just passed and stop before the next one reaches you. Standing still in a matching color while a light passes over you is the safest you can be.

Why do I keep getting caught in Mottle?

You usually get caught for one of three reasons: you moved too far in one go instead of using short dashes, you stopped on a patch of ground that does not match your color, or you ignored the searchlights and moved while one was on top of you. The fix is to shorten your trips, pick your stopping spots by color before you move, and wait for the lights to pass. Getting caught is almost always impatience — a shorter, more patient run survives longer than a greedy one.

Can you play Mottle on mobile or a phone?

Yes. Mottle supports both touch and mouse, so it plays the same on a phone or tablet as it does on a desktop. You drag with your finger to move and lift it to camouflage, and the timing of the searchlights is identical. It works in a browser with no install, so you can start on any device that can load the page.

Is Mottle multiplayer, or do you play against AI seekers?

Mottle is a single-player game — the seekers are part of the game's arena rather than other real players, so you are playing against the searchlights, the alert system, and the clock, not against other people online. That means no queue, no accounts, and no lag. You just load the game, hide, and chase fireflies as the rounds speed up.
